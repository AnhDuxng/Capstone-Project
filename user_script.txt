2. My presentation consists of six main sections, starting with the motivation and architectural shift from Phase 2 to Phase 3, moving through theoretical foundations, system design, implementation, and ending with our empirical evaluation and future work.

3. Today, paper diplomas and PDF scans are very easy to forge. Meanwhile, background checks are slow, manual, and rely on centralized university portals. Furthermore, when graduates present a diploma, they suffer from over-disclosure—exposing their date of birth, national ID, and GPA even when only the degree title is needed. Therefore, my goal is to build a decentralized solution that provides tamper-evidence, privacy-preserving selective disclosure, and cost-efficient scaling for large university cohorts.

4. In previous phases, we built a working prototype using SD-JWT credentials and on-chain verification. However, when we scaled up to a university cohort of 10,000 graduates, we faced severe bottlenecks. First, minting credentials directly on-chain consumed over 103,000 gas each, exceeding 1 billion gas for a single cohort. Second, pinning files to IPFS introduced recurring storage costs and gateway downtime. Finally, parsing SD-JWT required a custom library parser, adding unnecessary complexity.

5. To address these bottlenecks, Phase 3 defines clear objectives: eliminate mandatory IPFS dependencies by using self-contained JSON files; simplify cryptography with standard EIP-712 typed data signing; reduce on-chain gas overhead by at least 10 times via a single batch anchor; enable fast salted keccak256 selective disclosure; use an on-chain bitmap for compact revocation; and achieve high issuance throughput of over 500 credentials per second.

6. We moved from an expensive NFT-per-student model to a cryptographic attestation model. Instead of pushing large payload and metadata to IPFS and smart contracts, the blockchain now serves purely as a Trust Anchor storing only a single 32-byte Merkle root. All credential files are stored locally by students, completely removing recurring storage fees.

7. Comparing with existing solutions: MIT’s BlockCerts lacks selective disclosure, exposing all student personal data. Singapore’s OpenCerts uses on-chain mapping revocation, costing around 45,000 gas per revocation. Europe’s EBSI provides privacy but requires a private consortium network. Our BK Credential achieves the best balance: running on public Ethereum with native Salted Keccak-256 selective disclosure, and space-efficient bitmap revocation costing only 16.8 gas per credential.

8. Moving to theoretical foundations, our system follows the standard W3C trust triangle between Issuer, Holder, and Verifier. In Phase 2, SD-JWT caused integration issues because the ES256K curve is not supported by standard JWT libraries. In Phase 3, I adopted EIP-712 typed structured data. It provides native wallet signing, human-readable verification, and a secure Domain Separator that prevents cross-contract and cross-chain replay attacks.

9. The Phase 3 architecture rests on four technical building blocks: First, Salted Keccak-256 Commitments using abi.encode with length-prefixes to prevent hash collision. Second, Standard Merkle Trees for batch compression. Third, EIP-712 Typed Signing for tamper-proof credentials. And fourth, a 256-bit Bitmap Registry allowing 256 revocation flags in a single storage slot.

10.Here is our 3-tier system architecture. In the off-chain layer, the university signs credentials and builds the Merkle tree with zero gas expenditure. In the on-chain layer on Sepolia, we deploy two minimal contracts: IssuerRegistry to manage authorized signers, and CredentialRegistryV3 to anchor batches and track revocations. Finally, the application layer connects all stakeholders. Notice that only one single transaction is needed to anchor an entire graduating class.

11. The BkCredential struct cleanly separates Public Claims—such as degree title and graduation year—from Private Claims—such as student name, ID number, and GPA. Private attributes are represented strictly as 32-byte cryptographic commitments. When a student receives their credential file, it includes the EIP-712 signature, the Merkle proof, and salt disclosures, allowing them to selectively reveal any attribute.

12. Our smart contract design strictly follows the principle of separation of concerns. IssuerRegistry manages university signing authorities with two-step ownership transfer. CredentialRegistryV3 handles anchorBatch and bitmap revocations. Crucially, we enforce a cross-contract check: whenever a batch is anchored, CredentialRegistryV3 calls IssuerRegistry.isSigner to verify that the sender is authorized. We also integrated OpenZeppelin’s Pausable module as an emergency kill-switch.

13. The operational workflow is straightforward: For issuance, the administrator uploads a student CSV file. The backend validates schema, generates cryptographic salts, signs credentials using EIP-712, constructs the Merkle tree, and triggers the anchorBatch transaction on Sepolia. For revocation, when a degree must be canceled, the admin executes revoke, flipping a single bit in the on-chain bitmap, achieving immediate invalidation with minimal gas.

14. Regarding implementation: our smart contracts are built in Solidity with OpenZeppelin v5 and Hardhat. The web application is developed on Next.js 14 with TypeScript, Prisma, and PostgreSQL on Neon. To ensure a zero-friction experience for non-technical students, we integrated Privy passwordless email authentication, so students don’t need a crypto wallet. We also packaged the core cryptographic operations into a modular, 6-package TypeScript SDK named @bkcred/sdk.

15. To handle large cohorts without crashing the server, I engineered an asynchronous chunked issuance engine that divides batches into chunks of 500 credentials. Combined with an in-memory job queue and Server-Sent Events (SSE) streaming progress to the UI, the engine maintains smooth performance and strictly caps Node.js heap memory usage safely below 250 Megabytes, even when processing 10,000 credentials.

16. We provide four dedicated web portals: Issuer for university staff, Holder for students with selective disclosure toggles, Verifier for instant drag-and-drop validation, and Admin for signer lifecycle and revocation. At this point, I would like to invite the committee to watch a 5-minute live demonstration video showcasing the end-to-end operation across these four portals.

All 33 attacks were successfully rejected by our validation engine

19. On-chain cost validation is our proudest achievement. Because we anchor only a single Merkle root, the on-chain cost exhibits O(1) constant-time complexity. For a cohort of 10,000 students, gas consumption dropped from 240 million gas in Phase 2 down to 167,804 gas in Phase 3—representing a 1,430-fold reduction. In our live deployment on Ethereum Sepolia block 11,654,826, anchoring 10,000 diplomas cost only 0.00019 ETH, or roughly 56 cents, averaging 16.8 gas per student.

20. we honestly analyze our system limitations and engineering trade-offs. First, our binary signer kill-switch provides instant containment if a key leaks, though routine personnel turnover is currently handled off-chain. Second, credentials reside off-chain to avoid gas costs and protect student privacy, relying on standard institutional backups. Third, we omitted cryptographic holder binding to ensure zero-friction adoption for students without crypto wallets. And fourth, our commitments support binary disclosure rather than zero-knowledge range proofs to avoid heavy client-side computation

21. For future work, we see five promising directions: First, deploying on Ethereum Layer 2 rollups like Arbitrum or Base for another 50-to-100x cost reduction. Second, integrating Zero-Knowledge range proofs so students can prove their GPA is above 3.0 without exposing the exact grade. Third, aligning with W3C VC version 2.0 and DIDs for international accreditation. Fourth, engineering a native mobile wallet with Secure Enclave hardware storage. And finally, establishing a cross-university credential federation across Vietnamese institutions.
