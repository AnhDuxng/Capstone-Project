\section{Summary of Research Findings and Engineering Contributions}

The research and development conducted in this Capstone Project addressed the fundamental tension in decentralized academic credential systems: balancing cryptographic tamper-evidence, granular selective disclosure privacy, and high-throughput institutional usability without incurring prohibitive on-chain gas costs or external decentralized storage dependencies. By systematically evolving the system from the operational bottlenecks of Phase 2, this project developed, implemented, and empirically validated the \textbf{BK Credential System Phase 3} on the Ethereum Sepolia Testnet.

The primary research findings and engineering contributions of this work are synthesized across four foundational dimensions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Decentralized Architecture and Native Cryptographic Formulation}:
    The research established an off-chain typed signing model based on the Ethereum EIP-712 standard, eliminating dependencies on distributed file systems (IPFS) and unwieldy SD-JWT library parsing. By formulating attribute commitments as salted Keccak-256 hashes ($C_i = \text{keccak256}(\text{abi.encode}(\text{salt}_i, \text{key}_i, \text{value}_i))$), the architecture achieves strict cryptographic pre-image resistance and collision freedom while allowing graduates to selectively disclose individual claims (e.g., degree title, graduation year) without invalidating the registrar's digital signature.

    \item \textbf{Constant-Time $\mathcal{O}(1)$ Blockchain State Scaling}:
    By decoupling credential payload storage from on-chain state anchoring, the system compresses arbitrarily large academic cohorts into a single 32-byte cryptographic Merkle root anchored in \texttt{CredentialRegistryV3.sol}. Coupled with a 256-bit word binary bitmap revocation registry, on-chain state transitions scale with $\mathcal{O}(1)$ complexity. The live deployment on Ethereum Sepolia demonstrated an anchor cost of 167,804 gas for an entire 10,000-credential batch, translating to an amortized cost of only \textbf{16.78 gas per student credential}.

    \item \textbf{High-Throughput Issuance and Modular Full-Stack Engineering}:
    To bridge theoretical cryptography and practical registrar operations, the project delivered a modular 6-package TypeScript SDK and an asynchronous chunked issuance engine (\texttt{bulk.ts}, processing $20 \times 500 = 10,000$ credentials). Supported by an in-memory job queue with real-time Server-Sent Events (SSE) streaming, the issuance pipeline achieves a peak signing throughput of \textbf{809 credentials/second} and completes the end-to-end issuance of 10,000 verifiable diplomas in \textbf{27.54 seconds}, while capping Node.js V8 heap memory usage safely below 250~MB. Four specialized web portals (Issuer, Student Holder with Privy passwordless authentication, Verifier, and Admin) provide an intuitive interface for all stakeholders.

    \item \textbf{Empirical Verification Latency and Comprehensive Security Assurance}:
    Through exhaustive benchmark evaluation across Scenarios A through E, the canonical 10-step client-side verification pipeline demonstrated an average latency of \textbf{5.502 ms}, operating entirely within standard web browsers without recurring RPC gas expenditure. The smart contract suite achieved 100\% automated unit test coverage across all branches and functions (52 Hardhat tests), passed static vulnerability audits via Slither (0 high/medium severity findings), and successfully validated 33 out of 33 adversarial exploit simulations under a formal STRIDE threat model.
\end{enumerate}

\section{System Limitations and Engineering Trade-Offs}

While empirical benchmarks and static security audits confirm that the BK Credential System Phase 3 accomplishes its core research and engineering objectives, academic rigor demands an honest examination of system limitations, architectural trade-offs, and practical boundaries. In this section, we analyze the key trade-offs made during development and document the limitations of our current prototype.

\subsection{Signer Lifecycle and Revocation Consistency}
\label{sec:signer-lifecycle}

A central architectural design consideration and practical limitation identified during our system evaluation involves the semantics of signing key revocation in \texttt{IssuerRegistry.sol} versus verification enforcement in the 10-step pipeline. In institutional credential governance, signing key lifecycle management must satisfy two fundamentally conflicting operational requirements:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Emergency Key Compromise}: If an issuer's private key is leaked or compromised, the institution must immediately neutralize the key to halt unauthorized batch anchoring and ensure that verifiers reject fraudulent credentials signed by the compromised key.
    \item \textbf{Routine Administrative Turnover}: When an authorized signing officer (such as a University President, Dean, or Registrar) completes their official term of office, their signing privilege must be decommissioned for all future batches. However, tens of thousands of legitimate diplomas issued and signed during their active tenure must remain valid and verifiable in perpetuity.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Analysis of Implemented Phase 3 Prototype (Emergency Kill-Switch Pattern)}:
In the current prototype, \texttt{IssuerRegistry.sol} maintains signer authorization as a minimal binary state flag:
\begin{equation}
    \texttt{mapping(address => bool) public isSigner;}
\end{equation}
When \texttt{revokeSigner(address signer)} is invoked by the contract owner, \texttt{isSigner[signer]} is set to \texttt{false}. At verification time, Step~7 queries \texttt{isSigner(recoveredAddress)}. If the flag is \texttt{false}, verification immediately halts with a \texttt{SIGNER\_NOT\_REGISTERED} error.

\textbf{Engineering Rationale}: This binary pattern was intentionally adopted for Phase~3 for three reasons:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Minimal Gas Overhead}: Updating a single storage slot requires an efficient warm SSTORE consuming only $\sim$28,000 gas.
    \item \textit{Immediate Containment}: It provides an instantaneous, network-wide emergency kill-switch that neutralizes compromised keys without requiring verifiers to compute temporal interval arithmetic.
    \item \textit{Immunity to Timestamp Manipulation}: It avoids reliance on EVM block timestamps (\texttt{block.timestamp}), thereby eliminating any dependency on miner/validator timestamp manipulation boundaries (SWC-116).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Identified Operational Limitation}: Under this binary design, invoking \texttt{revokeSigner} upon an official's routine retirement or reassignment would inadvertently cause historically legitimate diplomas previously signed by that officer to fail Step~7 verification. Consequently, in our current operational prototype, routine personnel transitions are managed by safely decommissioning the private key from the issuance server's active keystore rather than triggering the on-chain emergency kill-switch.

\paragraph{Architectural Upgrade: Temporal Validity Window Pattern}:
To natively decouple emergency key containment from routine personnel turnover within the smart contract layer, the registry architecture can be extended with timestamped validity intervals:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Solidity, basicstyle=\small\ttfamily, frame=single, caption={Proposed Struct for Timestamped Signer Governance}, label={lst:signer-struct}]
struct SignerRecord {
    bool active;          // True if currently authorized to anchor new batches
    uint64 assignedAt;    // Unix timestamp when authorization commenced
    uint64 revokedAt;     // Unix timestamp when revoked (0 if currently active)
    bool isCompromised;   // True if revoked under emergency key leakage
}
mapping(address => SignerRecord) public signerRegistry;
\end{lstlisting}

Under this enhanced model, verification coordinates Step~7 (Signer Authorization) with Step~8 (Batch Anchor Validation):
\begin{equation}
    \text{Valid}(C) \iff \neg \text{signers}[s].\text{isCompromised} \land (\text{assignedAt} \le \text{anchoredAt}) \land (\text{revokedAt} == 0 \lor \text{anchoredAt} < \text{revokedAt})
\end{equation}
where $\text{anchoredAt}$ represents the immutable block timestamp of the Merkle batch anchor retrieved from \texttt{CredentialRegistryV3} in Step~8. In routine turnover, invoking \texttt{retireSigner(signer)} sets $\texttt{active} = \texttt{false}$ and $\texttt{revokedAt} = \texttt{block.timestamp}$, ensuring that historical diplomas anchored during the official's legitimate tenure remain valid indefinitely while preventing any new batches from being created. Conversely, if a key is compromised, invoking \texttt{flagCompromised(signer)} sets $\texttt{isCompromised} = \texttt{true}$, triggering an immediate, retroactive invalidation across all verification queries.

\subsection{Off-Chain Credential Storage and Availability Boundary}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Identified Limitation}: Because the blockchain registry only persists a 32-byte Merkle root for each batch, the full credential payload, individual salted claim values, and Merkle sibling hashes reside exclusively off-chain (held by the student graduate or stored in the university's relational database). If a student loses their issued credential JSON file and the issuing institution suffers catastrophic database loss without backups, the on-chain anchor cannot independently reconstruct the graduate's diploma data.
    \item \textbf{Engineering Trade-Off}: Storing complete credential payloads on-chain or pinning multi-megabyte JSON trees to decentralized storage (such as IPFS) incurs significant recurring gas fees, pinning costs, or privacy leaks for sensitive academic records. Offloading payload storage to students and institutional vaults preserves data confidentiality and achieves our target $\sim$16.8 gas/credential efficiency, while placing data retention responsibility on conventional institutional backup policies.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Absence of Holder-Bound Presentation Proofs}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Identified Limitation}: Individual credentials exist as portable, self-contained JSON files. While cryptographic signatures prevent any alteration of credential attributes or grades, the current architecture does not enforce a cryptographic holder-binding challenge during presentation. Consequently, if a student transfers their JSON file to an unauthorized party, that party could present the credential as their own unless the verifier independently checks the subject's identity.
    \item \textbf{Engineering Trade-Off}: Enforcing cryptographic holder binding would require every graduating student to possess an active Web3 wallet and generate client-side ECDSA presentation signatures. To ensure zero-friction adoption for non-technical graduates, Phase~3 opted for passwordless email authentication (Privy). Future iterations can introduce optional holder DID presentation proofs for high-assurance workflows.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Selective Disclosure Functional Boundaries vs. Zero-Knowledge Proofs}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Limitation}: Our salted-hash commitment scheme ($\text{keccak256}(\text{abi.encode}(\text{salt}, \text{key}, \text{value}))$) is limited to binary claim disclosure—meaning an attribute is either revealed in full plaintext or withheld entirely. It does not support numerical range predicates (such as proving $\text{GPA} \ge 3.0$ or $\text{Age} \ge 21$ without disclosing the exact numerical value).
    \item \textbf{Engineering Trade-Off}: Implementing zero-knowledge range proofs (e.g., via Circom/SnarkJS or Bulletproofs) would require students to download multi-megabyte proving keys and execute client-side proving computations consuming several seconds and substantial browser RAM. Salted Keccak-256 commitments offer a sub-millisecond, zero-dependency alternative that satisfies the vast majority of academic verification needs.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Absence of Human-Subject Usability Evaluation}
While the technical performance, throughput, and security of the system were thoroughly validated across automated test harnesses (52 unit tests, 10,000 synthetic credentials, and 33 exploit simulations), the current project scope did not include formal human-subject user studies. Specifically, empirical metrics such as System Usability Scale (SUS) scores, user error rates during selective disclosure configuration, and administrative onboarding friction among non-technical university registrars remain to be quantified in future operational trials.

\subsection{Memory and Concurrency Constraints in Monolithic Environments}
Processing large cohorts in Node.js requires careful memory management. Although our chunked issuance engine ($20 \times 500 = 10,000$) successfully caps V8 heap memory usage under 250~MB, the current job queue operates in-memory via \texttt{BulkJobManager}. For production multi-tenant deployments processing concurrent cohorts exceeding 50,000 credentials, the job manager should be migrated to an out-of-process distributed queue (such as Redis-backed BullMQ) to ensure persistence across server restarts.

\section{Future Directions and Open Research Opportunities}

From the lessons learned and empirical insights gained during our project, we have identified several promising avenues that future student cohorts or research initiatives can explore:

\subsection{Deployment on Ethereum Layer 2 Rollups (Arbitrum, Base, Optimism)}
While our Phase 3 architecture already reduces on-chain anchoring costs to $\sim$167,804 gas on Sepolia, migrating the smart contract registry suite to Ethereum Layer 2 (L2) Rollups (such as Arbitrum One, Base, or Optimism) would decrease transaction costs by another $50 \sim 100\times$, lowering cohort anchoring fees to negligible fractions of a cent. Furthermore, Layer 2 networks offer sub-second transaction finality, enabling near-instantaneous anchor confirmations for university registrars.

\subsection{Zero-Knowledge Range Proofs and Predicate Verification}
Our current salted-hash commitment scheme supports binary claim disclosure. An exciting extension would be integrating succinct Zero-Knowledge Range Proofs (e.g., using Bulletproofs or Circom/SnarkJS) to support numerical predicate evaluations without revealing raw data. For instance, a job applicant could cryptographically prove that their Cumulative GPA satisfies $\text{GPA} \ge 3.2$ or that their age satisfies $\text{Age} \ge 18$, without ever revealing their exact GPA or birthdate to prospective employers.

\subsection{Harmonization with W3C Verifiable Credentials Data Model v2.0}
To achieve international accreditation interoperability, our credential data structures can be directly harmonized with the W3C Verifiable Credentials Data Model v2.0~\cite{W3CVC} and W3C Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs)~\cite{w3c_did}. By supporting standard DID resolvers (such as \texttt{did:ethr} and \texttt{did:web}), academic credentials issued by Ho Chi Minh City University of Technology (HCMUT) could be automatically recognized and verified by global academic platforms, including the European Digital Credentials for Learning (EDCI).

\subsection{Mobile Holder Wallet Application with Hardware Enclave Security}
Another valuable project continuation would be engineering a dedicated mobile wallet app (using React Native or Flutter) for iOS and Android. By utilizing the smartphone's hardware Secure Enclave or Android Keystore, student graduates could securely store their private credential presentations, authenticate presentations with biometric FaceID/TouchID, and present verifiable diplomas offline via encrypted QR codes or Near Field Communication (NFC) during in-person interviews.

\subsection{Cross-Institutional Academic Credential Federation}
Because our \texttt{IssuerRegistry.sol} smart contract is architected as a multi-tenant registry, it can naturally serve as the backbone for an academic consortium across Vietnamese universities (such as VNU-HCM member universities, VNU Hanoi, and Hanoi University of Science and Technology). Under this consortium, each member university could register its own signing keys, enabling decentralized credit transfers, dual-degree verifications, and joint diploma validations without relying on any centralized intermediary or government server.

\section{Concluding Remarks}

Through the design, formalization, and implementation of the BK Credential System Phase 3, we have demonstrated that decentralized, blockchain-backed academic credential verification can be achieved with high throughput, minimal operational cost, and robust privacy preservation. By replacing the complex SD-JWT and IPFS-dependent architecture with off-chain EIP-712 structured data signing and Merkle tree batch anchoring, our proposed system effectively eliminates the traditional trade-off between cryptographic decentralization and institutional operational feasibility.

With an amortized cost of only \textbf{16.8 gas per graduate} at university cohort scale, a sub-6 millisecond offline verification latency, and mathematically verifiable integrity guarantees, this capstone project delivers a practical engineering solution that addresses the pressing needs of modern educational institutions. Working on this project has not only deepened our understanding of applied cryptography, EVM smart contracts, and full-stack software engineering, but also provided us with valuable experience in balancing theoretical cryptographic guarantees with the realities of production software architecture.
